1The building blocks

Probability

The probability of an event D is written P(D). It's a number between 0 and 1 (0% to 100%) that describes how likely D is to happen.

Conditional probability

The probability of D given that another event T has already happened is written

P(D \mid T)

and read as "the probability of D given T." The order matters: P(D \mid T) and P(T \mid D) are usually very different numbers. Mixing them up is the whole reason false positives are so surprising.

Notation

Throughout this page we use four events. A prime (′) means "not."

D
The person has the condition
D'
The person doesn't have the condition
T
The test result is positive
T'
The test result is negative

2Bayes' theorem

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}

For any two events A and B with P(B) \neq 0, Bayes' theorem lets us flip a conditional probability around.

A test's sensitivity and specificity tell us how likely a result is given the condition. What a patient wants to know is the reverse: how likely the condition is given the result. Bayes' theorem, combined with the prevalence, gets us from one to the other.

3What you enter

Prevalence

P(D)

The share of the population that has the condition at a given time. The share that doesn't is its complement:

P(D') = 1 - P(D)

Prevalence is usually estimated by sampling: test a group of people, count the true cases, and divide by the size of the group.

Sensitivity

P(T \mid D)

How often the test comes back positive for someone who has the condition, also called the true positive rate. A highly sensitive test rarely misses a real case.

Specificity

P(T' \mid D')

How often the test comes back negative for someone who doesn't have the condition, also called the true negative rate. A highly specific test rarely raises a false alarm.

The false-positive and false-negative rates are the complements of specificity and sensitivity: P(T \mid D') = 1 - P(T' \mid D') and P(T' \mid D) = 1 - P(T \mid D).

5What the calculator shows

Probability of testing positive

P(+)

Someone can test positive in two ways: they have the condition and the test catches it, or they don't have it and the test raises a false alarm. These can't both happen, so we add them:

P(T) = P(T \mid D)\,P(D) + P(T \mid D')\,P(D')

Probability of testing negative

P(−)

In the same way, a negative result is either a correct all-clear or a missed case:

P(T') = P(T' \mid D')\,P(D') + P(T' \mid D)\,P(D)

Positive predictive value

PPV

This is the number most people actually care about: if I test positive, how likely is it that I have the condition?

It's easy to confuse this with sensitivity. A test with 95% sensitivity does not mean a positive result is 95% likely to be right. When a condition is rare, false positives from the large healthy group can far outnumber the true positives.

PPV is the share of all positive results that are true positives:

\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{true positives}}{\text{true positives} + \text{false positives}}

In terms of what you enter, writing \text{Se} for sensitivity, \text{Sp} for specificity and p for prevalence:

\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{Se} \cdot p}{\text{Se} \cdot p + (1 - \text{Sp})(1 - p)}

Written with our notation, the denominator is exactly P(T), and Bayes' theorem shows that PPV is the probability of having the condition given a positive test:

\begin{aligned} \text{PPV} &= \frac{P(T \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(T \mid D)\,P(D) + P(T \mid D')\,P(D')} \\[6pt] &= \frac{P(T \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(T)} = P(D \mid T) \end{aligned}

False discovery rate

FDR

The share of positive results that are wrong: the person tested positive but doesn't have the condition. It's the complement of PPV:

\text{FDR} = 1 - \text{PPV} = 1 - P(D \mid T) = P(D' \mid T)

Negative predictive value

NPV

The mirror image of PPV: if I test negative, how likely is it that I'm actually clear? Just as sensitivity isn't PPV, a test with 95% specificity doesn't mean a negative result is 95% likely to be right.

\text{NPV} = \frac{\text{true negatives}}{\text{true negatives} + \text{false negatives}}

Using the same shorthand:

\text{NPV} = \frac{\text{Sp} \cdot (1 - p)}{\text{Sp} \cdot (1 - p) + (1 - \text{Se}) \cdot p}
\begin{aligned} \text{NPV} &= \frac{P(T' \mid D')\,P(D')}{P(T' \mid D')\,P(D') + P(T' \mid D)\,P(D)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{P(T' \mid D')\,P(D')}{P(T')} = P(D' \mid T') \end{aligned}

False omission rate

FOR

The share of negative results that are wrong: the person tested negative but does have the condition. It's the complement of NPV:

\text{FOR} = 1 - \text{NPV} = 1 - P(D' \mid T') = P(D \mid T')

6A note about assumptions

These calculations assume the condition is spread evenly through the population, so that P(D) is the same for everyone. In reality it rarely is: symptoms, exposure and risk factors all change an individual's chances, which is why a doctor's judgment matters.

The core lesson still holds, though. A test with 95% sensitivity does not mean there's a 95% chance you have the condition if you test positive. Often the chance is much lower.
